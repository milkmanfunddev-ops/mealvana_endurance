<blockquote style="background: orange">
💡 <strong>Theme 1. First contact with a new AI feature requires guided onboarding, users default to the familiar</strong>
</blockquote>

When users encounter an AI-forward interface for the first time, and especially when they are still orienting to a new feature, they naturally gravitate toward what feels familiar, which is browsing visible options rather than initiating a conversation.

Across all 6 sessions, no participant used the chat feature as their first move. This is telling us that the interaction model is unfamiliar enough to need a proper introduction. **Jeff**, who said the chatbot felt less intuitive at first, also said he thought he would get used to it and enjoy it. The pattern here is about first contact, not long-term fit.

`<speaks directly to Xuan's design>`

An AI-forward design likely needs its own dedicated onboarding flow that walks users through the chat interaction before they land in the feature, and needs to be patient while users build comfort and trust with conversational input over time.

Consider a short guided introduction specific to this feature, something that shows users what the chat is for and what a first message might look like, before they are left to discover it on their own.

[image: Screenshot "5a1c6700-c77f-4108-b5f5-6b990564b971.png" — illustrative screenshot accompanying Theme 1 (likely a screen from Xuan's chat-first prototype as seen by a first-time tester).]

---

<blockquote style="background: orange">
💡 <strong>Theme 2. Users want to be told what to eat! But customization has to be smooth and powerful.</strong>
</blockquote>

Many users expressed real relief at the idea of a generated plan they could simply follow. But the condition is clear: if the suggested recipes include things users would never eat, trust in the whole system collapses immediately.

`<speaks directly to Lee's design>`

The "quick tweak" buttons in Lee's design were the closest thing to solving this well, with the lightweight adjustments without starting over. That direction is right and needs to go further. Customization must feel effortless and capable, not like a separate chore on top of the planning itself.

The generate-first model is validated. The next challenge is making the customization layer robust enough that users trust the generated plan as a real starting point, not a placeholder they have to fix before it becomes usable.

[image: Screenshot "Screenshot_2026-05-20_at_6.01.03_PM.png" — screenshot accompanying Theme 2, likely showing the "quick tweak" buttons in Lee's design.]

---

<blockquote style="background: orange">
💡 <strong>Theme 3. Every user is already struggling. Our app is not competing with some other good planners</strong>
</blockquote>

All 6 participants described disorganized, reactive, or absent meal planning before the session. None had a structured weekly practice.

They are athletes who care about performance but have no real system connecting food to training. The app is competing with "whatever's in the fridge," not with other apps. The design must require as little prior planning skill or motivation as possible to get started.

[image: Screenshot "Screenshot_2026-05-20_at_6.02.05_PM.png" — screenshot accompanying Theme 3.]

---

<blockquote style="background: orange">
💡 <strong>Theme 4. Lowering activation energy is the real product promise</strong>
</blockquote>

The barrier is the effort of starting. Users described the mental cost of deciding, listing, and shopping as the thing that makes their planning system collapse every week.

An app that generates a complete plan first and makes editing optional removes this barrier. Asking users to build from a blank slate adds friction at exactly the wrong moment. Show them something good first. Let them react to it.

[image: Screenshot "Screenshot_2026-05-20_at_6.02.48_PM.png" — screenshot accompanying Theme 4.]

---

<blockquote style="background: orange">
💡 <strong>Theme 5. Personalization is only believed when the system shows what it already knows</strong>
</blockquote>

User sense of how personalized a design felt was tied directly to visible proof that the system already had information about them, not just to how many choices they were given.

When the app surfaced training context, upcoming race, or recovery day labels alongside meal suggestions, users noticed and responded positively. Without that visible proof, the recommendations just feel like random suggestions. Personalization has to be shown, not implied.

[image: Screenshot "Screenshot_2026-05-20_at_6.03.33_PM.png" — screenshot accompanying Theme 5.]

## Also worth noting

<blockquote style="background: green">
💡 Also worth noting

1. <strong>Visual richness and food imagery drive engagement — especially on first impression</strong>

**Ashley** was the only participant to rate Xuan's design higher on engagement than Lee's (4 vs. 3), and she was explicit about why: food photography, color, and visual appeal. She described Lee's design as "less visual" and "sleek" but docked it a point for engagement compared to Xuan's more image-rich presentation. Both designs scored equally on ease of use (5/5) and Ashley rated Lee's higher on personalization, so the gap is specifically about visual experience. This is a concrete opportunity: Lee's structural model could absorb Xuan's visual richness — food images, color coding, appetite-driven aesthetics — without changing the interaction model at all.
</blockquote>

<blockquote style="background: green">
💡 Also worth noting

1. <strong>Macro numbers must be visible, clearly labeled, and tied to a daily goal</strong>

Users make food decisions based on hitting targets, not on recipe appeal. But the P / C / F shorthand confused multiple participants, and several wanted to see progress toward a daily total — not just per-meal figures in isolation. The numbers are motivating when they make sense. They create friction when they don't.
</blockquote>

<blockquote style="background: green">
💡 Also worth noting

<strong>3. The shopping list is the magic moment, but it only lands if the flow before it works</strong>

Every participant who reached the shopping list reacted positively. The excitement came from the feeling that the list built itself from their choices. Organized by store section rather than by meal was the preferred format. Store-specific integration and grocery pickup came up unprompted.
</blockquote>

<blockquote style="background: green">
💡 Also worth noting

<strong>4. Recipe diversity is a trust and inclusion gap, not just a content gap.</strong>

**Madhumita**, **Anna**, and **Cherie** all surfaced missing options: vegetarian, South Asian food traditions, ingredient-level swaps within recipes. If a user opens the app and cannot find food they actually eat, the personalization promise breaks immediately regardless of how good the design is.
</blockquote>
